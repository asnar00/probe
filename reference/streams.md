# Streams — a design note (2026-08-29)

A stream is a value over time: a partially resident array of `(t, v)` with `t` monotonically increasing. A program over streams is an SSA of streams — each operation a node whose inputs and outputs arrive over time — and the lower-level things the project already has (fibres, tasks, the timer, the UART interrupt, chunks, views) are what such a program runs on. The claim to test is that the abstract entity and that machinery are *congruent*: the stream's residency is a ring buffer, its producers are handlers or tasks, its consumers are tasks woken by data or by the clock, and a stream program's schedule is the scheduler's. This note starts from cases, because the model should be forced by needs rather than chosen.

## Cases

Six, each something the project or a real machine wants. For each: what arrives, how often, what the program does with it, what it must never do.

**1. Keys (irregular, sparse, latency-bound).** The UART delivers a byte at a time, whenever a person types; `os/echo.ssa` pushes it from the interrupt handler into `keys: u8$` and `read` sleeps until a newline is among the unread bytes (done, 2026-08-29). As a stream: `keys: u8~` with irregular `t`; the program reads it line by line (a window ended by a delimiter), and echoes. What matters: nothing is lost between interrupts (the ring has room — bounded, declared), and the consumer runs soon after the byte, not on a tick. Two items may carry the same `t` (a burst).

**2. The clock (regular, sparse).** `tick: ()~` at 20 Hz — a stream with no value, only times — is what `os/clock.ssa` and `os/tasks.ssa` are built on. As a stream it is the simplest regular one: `t_i = t_0 + i/20`, so `t` is implicit and the stream is an index. What matters: lateness is measured, not assumed (the sleep tests already print it); a missed tick is a fact the program can see, not a silent gap.

**3. Audio (regular, dense, block-processed).** 48 000 `f32` a second in, a filter, 48 000 out. Nobody handles these one at a time: the device delivers a block of 64 or 256 samples and wants a block back within the block's time. As a stream: `in: f32~48000`, and a biquad is `y[n] = b0 x[n] + b1 x[n-1] + b2 x[n-2] - a1 y[n-1] - a2 y[n-2]` — an operation that reads *behind* itself on both streams by a bounded amount. What matters: the block is a view into the ring (a contiguous `f32[]` — chunks apply, `fadd .4s` does the work), the two-sample history survives across blocks, and the deadline is the block period: this is where "static bounds on how long emitted code takes" (noted with `at`/`after`) comes back.

**4. Video (regular, each item an array).** 30 frames a second, each an `f32[,]` (or `u8[,,]`), a per-pixel operation, out. As a stream: `frames: f32[,]~30` — a stream whose *elements* are views; the per-frame operation is the array layer, already there (`mul out, frame, gain`), applied per arrival. What matters: a frame is big, so the ring holds two or three, not many; and a frame that is not done by the next arrival is dropped or the pipeline lags — a policy the program states, not the runtime.

**5. Pointer input to a regular signal (irregular in, regular out).** A mouse or touch reports position when it moves; the program wants position at 60 Hz for drawing, smoothed. As streams: `moves: point~` irregular, `pos: point~60` regular, `pos = resample moves, 60` with sample-and-hold, then a moving average over the last 5. What matters: resampling is a *library* operation with a stated rule (hold, or interpolate), and a window is "the last N items" or "the last T seconds", which are different things on an irregular stream and the same thing on a regular one.

**6. A sensor with windowed statistics (irregular, long windows).** A temperature every few seconds, not quite periodic; the program keeps the max and mean over the last minute and raises a flag when the max crosses a line. As a stream: `temps: fixed~` irregular, `hi: fixed~ = max (window temps, 60 s)`, `flag: u1~ = cmp.gt hi, limit` — a reduction over a time window, then an elementwise op. What matters: the window's residency is bounded by rate × duration, which the program can declare and the compiler can check (the arena-capacity spirit); and the output stream is as irregular as the input (one item per input) unless the program asks for a rate.

Two things the cases have in common that are not obvious from the abstraction: **every stream has a declared bound on what is resident** (a ring of N, a window of T at rate R), and **the output's timing is a stated policy** (per input, at a rate, by a deadline) — never inferred.

## The model the cases force

- **A stream of T is a sequence of `(t, v)` with `t` non-decreasing** (equal `t` allowed: a burst). `t` is a `time` (`lib/time.ssa`: a rational of seconds — exact, so 3/3 and 7/7 of a second meet, as the sleep OS already relies on).
- **Regular streams carry their rate, not their times.** `f32~48000`: `t_i = t_0 + i/48000`; the stream is an array indexed by `i`, and its resident part is a contiguous view — exactly a slice, which is why chunks and every vector rule apply. **Irregular streams carry a `t` per item**: `point~`; the resident part is a ring of `(t, v)`.
- **Residency is a ring buffer with a declared capacity.** A stream value *is* the ring plus the position of "now": a buffer (header, elements) with a head and a tail — the same buffer that slices are views into, with two more words. A **frame** is the state at a time: for a regular stream the block since the last frame, for an irregular one the items since the last frame, or the latest value (sample-and-hold) — the program says which.
- **Operations lift.** Elementwise operations over streams of one rate are the slice operations on each block — no new definitions; on irregular streams, at each event, the other operand sampled-and-held (the rule stated once, in the library). **Windowed reductions** (`sum`, `max`, `mean` over the last N or the last T) are reductions over a view of the ring. **Delay** (`x[n-1]`) is a view one back. **Resample** turns irregular into regular by a rule; **merge** interleaves two streams by `t`. All library, over `number~` and `number~R` with the tower doing what it did for slices.
- **The plumbing is what exists.** A producer is an interrupt handler (`__irq`: the UART, the timer, a DMA-complete) or a task pushing into the ring; a consumer is a task that sleeps until the ring has what it needs (`at`/`after`, the timer) or is woken by the producer (`reschedule`); a regular dense stream is consumed a block at a time on the block's deadline; the SSA of streams is a graph whose edges are rings and whose nodes are tasks, and *scheduling it is the scheduler's job* — which is the congruence claim, made concrete: fibres for nodes that share a thread, tasks with the timer for those with deadlines, cores for those that can run apart.

## What it would take in the IR

Little that is new in kind. A stream type (`T~` irregular, `T~R` regular), which is a buffer with a header and two positions — a struct, dissolved like a slice. Operations to read a frame as a view (`frame s` → the block since last time, `window s, N` / `window s, 1 s` → the last N or the last T as a view), to push (`push s, v` or `push s, block`), to read time (`at s` → the `t` of the frame's first item; `now()` exists). Elementwise operations over regular streams as the slice operations on frames, via the library. A program over streams is then an ordinary program that runs a loop (or is a task the OS schedules), and the "SSA of streams" is the shape of that loop's body — every value in it a frame of some stream — which the compiler can check for bounds (a window never larger than the ring) and, later, for time (a node's body within its period). The new *machinery* would be the scheduling: a way to say that a program is a graph of nodes with rates and deadlines, and to have the OS programs' scheduler run it — `os/sleep.ssa` with a stream per task is not far from it.

## Open questions

1. **Whose time?** One time base for a program (the machine's `now()`, `time` seconds) with regular streams' `t_0` on it — or each stream its own index and time only where needed? I lean one base: the cases that mix streams (5, 6) need it, and the OS already has it.
2. **Frame or item?** Is the unit a program sees a *frame* (a view of what arrived since last time — regular streams get blocks, irregular ones get a possibly-empty list) or an *item* with the frame as a library on top? I lean frame: it is what the devices give, and an item is a frame of one.
3. **Where does the program run?** A stream program as a plain function that a task calls once per frame (the OS's job to call it on time), or as a declared graph the compiler turns into tasks? The first is honest and immediate; the second is the "SSA of streams" made real, and needs the OS scheduler to grow toward a dataflow one. I lean first, then second, with the same programs.
4. **Bounds and deadlines: declared, checked, or measured?** Rings and windows can be declared and checked statically now (the arena spirit). A node's time cannot yet — it can be *measured* (the boot tests do) and, later, bounded (the noted WCET idea). Declared-and-measured first.
5. **Dropping.** When a consumer lags a regular stream (case 4), the choice — drop, lag, or fail a check — is the program's. Which is the default, and is it per stream?

A first step that answers most of these by construction: cases 1 and 2 rewritten as streams (`os/echo.ssa` and `os/clock.ssa` reading `keys` and `tick`), then case 3 on a machine with a synthetic source, with the rings, frames and windows as `lib/stream.ssa` over buffers and views, and the OS calling the program once per frame.

## Time as an axis (2026-08-29, later)

"A good way of thinking about streams is a bit like arrays which take `x[int]`, except streams are `x[time]`. If a stream has metadata `dt`, then `x[t]` returns `x[t/dt]`. We can add interpolation rules — nearest neighbour, bilinear — analogous to texture samplers in the spatial domain. Ranges and slices also make sense." (ash)

This reshapes the model above in a good way: **sampling becomes a property of an axis**, not of streams. An axis of a view carries, beyond its count and stride, an origin and a step — `c = c₀ + i·dc` — and may be indexed by a coordinate: `x[t]` is `i = (t − t₀)/dt` and then a *rule* for a non-integer `i`: nearest, hold (floor: sample-and-hold), linear (the two neighbours weighted). On a regular stream that is `x[t]`; on an image with two such axes it is bilinear filtering; a texture is a view with samplers. Coordinates on a time axis are `time` (rational seconds), so `t/dt` is exact when `dt` is.

An **irregular** stream is an axis whose coordinates are not computed but stored — a `t` per item beside the value — and indexing by `t` is a search (binary; the `t`s are sorted by construction) followed by the same rules: hold gives the last value before `t`, linear interpolates. Regular and irregular differ only in whether a coordinate is computed or looked up. **Ranges by coordinate** follow: `window s, t₁, t₂` is a view of the samples with `t₁ ≤ t < t₂` — a contiguous block on a regular stream, the items in range on an irregular one; `frame` is "since last time".

What stays separate, and should: residency and plumbing. The ring is where a stream's window lives; reading outside the resident range is a `check` (or the drop policy); tasks, handlers and the timer feed it. The sampling model says what `x[t]` means; the ring says which `t` are answerable now.

In the IR: `s: f32~ = stream b, 48000` (a ring seen through a time axis); `x: f32 = sample s, t` (nearest by default) and `sample s, t, linear` (the rule an operand, like a texture's sampler state); `w: f32[] = window s, t1, t2`; and `p: f32 = sample img, x, y` on a spatial view with the same operation (done, bilinear, `lib/sample.ssa`, 2026-08-29). Open: whether the rule lives per read (an operand — the leaning: one stream is read both ways) or per stream (metadata with a default), and the edge rules — clamp, wrap, or fail; a ring's edges are "not yet" and "no longer", which want `check` by default.

## Ticks (2026-08-29, later still)

`probe cost` put a number on the rational-time design: a push into a regular stream cost some 300 000 SSA operations, a 128-bit rational multiply-and-add per item to fill a times buffer a regular stream never needs. ash: "compile out the time stuff except where necessary... or store times as integer tick-counts and only convert to seconds when necessary." Both at once: a ring has a **clock** (a rate in hz) and its items sit at **integer ticks** of it — what the machine's counter and a device's sample index are; an irregular ring stores a tick per item (`i64`), a regular one stores none (`t0 + k·step`). `time` is the boundary type: a tick becomes seconds by one division (`time_at`), a time becomes ticks by one multiplication (`sample`, `window`), once per call and never per item, and two rings on different clocks meet by one such conversion at the point they meet. The per-item paths simply do not mention `time` — which is (1) achieved by the library's structure rather than by an analysis. A push is ~500 SSA operations now, most of them the slide when the ring fills.
